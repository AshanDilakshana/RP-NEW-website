01 Introduction to the Research Domain

Aviation meteorology is a specialized discipline dedicated to measuring, observing, and predicting atmospheric phenomena that directly impact the safety, efficiency, and economics of civil and military air operations. Unlike broad public synoptic forecasts that cover hundreds of square kilometers over multiple days, aerodrome meteorological services require hyper-local (microscale to mesoscale) precision over short temporal horizons (0 to 3 hours).

This research lies at the intersection of Atmospheric Physics, Aeronautical Operations, and Applied Machine Learning. It investigates how data-driven AI models can ingest airport-level observations, learn complex non-linear meteorological interactions, output verified short-term forecasts, and directly translate them into operational safety indicators for pilots, air traffic controllers (ATC), and dispatchers.

02 Background of Aviation Meteorology

Aircraft flight dynamics and airport ground operations are governed by physical characteristics of the surrounding atmosphere. Key aerodrome parameters include:

  • Barometric Pressure (QNH): The altimeter sub-scale setting that causes the altimeter to read aerodrome elevation when on the ground. Pressure drops reduce air density and alter aircraft performance.
  • Surface Wind Speed and Direction: Determines runway selection, maximum permissible takeoff and landing crosswind components, and windshear risk.
  • Air Temperature & Dew Point: Determines air density, engine thrust output, takeoff roll distance, and the saturation point at which fog or low stratus clouds form.
  • Horizontal Visibility & Cloud Ceiling: Dictates whether aircraft operate under Visual Flight Rules (VFR) or Instrument Flight Rules (IFR), determining aerodrome landing minimums.

03 Aviation Weather Forecasting

In international civil aviation, standardized weather dissemination is governed by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO Annex 3) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO):

  • METAR (Meteorological Aerodrome Report): Routine hourly or half-hourly observation describing present ground-level weather at the airport.
  • TAF (Terminal Aerodrome Forecast): Human-forecaster-generated aerodrome forecast issued typically every 6 hours for a 24- to 30-hour validity window.

While TAFs provide structured broad guidance, operational crews frequently face sudden meso-beta scale convective events, gust fronts, and rapid visibility deterioration within the immediate 1 to 3 hours between TAF issuances. This critical window represents the focus of nowcasting and short-term hyper-local forecasting.

04 Weather Forecast Verification

A forecast without systematic verification provides unknown reliability. In aviation, forecast verification is the process of matching past predictive outputs with the actual observations that materialized at the targeted valid time.

Verification establishes quantitative credibility through statistical evaluation metrics:

  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Measures the average magnitude of absolute forecast errors, expressing precision in original units (e.g., knots, °C, hPa).
  • Root Mean Square Error (RMSE): Penalizes large individual outlier errors heavily, essential for detecting dangerous unexpected weather extremes.
  • Coefficient of Determination (R²): Measures the proportion of variance in the observed weather variable explained by the machine learning model.

05 Sri Lankan Weather Patterns and Monsoons

Sri Lanka's climate is characterized by two distinct monsoon seasons and two transitionary inter-monsoon periods:

Southwest Monsoon (May to September)

Persistent, high-velocity maritime airflow from the Indian Ocean, characterized by frequent rain bands, strong gusting winds, and hazardous crosswind conditions at VCBI's Runway 04/22.

Northeast Monsoon (December to February)

Continental wind flow from the Asian landmass bringing cooler and drier air, generating sharp diurnal temperature fluctuations, rapid barometric changes, and notable density altitude shifts.

First Inter-Monsoon (March to April)

Weak pressure gradients and light, shifting winds coupled with intense surface solar heating. Strong afternoon convection causes sudden cloud-base drops and localized squall lines.

Second Inter-Monsoon (October to November)

High relative humidity, widespread cloudiness, and low barometric pressures, frequently producing early morning radiation fog and severe tropical thunderstorm complexes over western Sri Lanka.

06 Literature Survey / Literature Review

The theoretical framework of this research is constructed upon an extensive review of international and Sri Lankan literature in machine learning, weather nowcasting, and aviation operations:

Key Academic Studies Reviewed

  1. Nagahamulla, Ratnayake, & Ratnaweera (2011) [1]: Demonstrated the capability of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) for predicting monsoon rainfall patterns in Sri Lanka using teleconnection indices, confirming that seasonal climatic behavior contains non-linear patterns amenable to neural networks.
  2. Hennayake, Dinalankara, & Mudunkotuwa (2021) [2]: Applied supervised machine learning algorithms to short-term general weather prediction in Sri Lanka, focusing on macro-scale precipitation and temperature rather than aerodrome-specific parameters.
  3. Maduranga & Edirisinghe (2021) [3]: Examined the climatology of convective lightning activity specifically over Bandaranaike International Airport (VCBI), underscoring the severe operational impacts of localized convective systems.
  4. Gultepe et al. (2019) [4]: Published an exhaustive international review on high-impact aviation weather, highlighting that horizontal visibility, ceiling, and microscale wind gusts are the primary drivers of airport-level disruption and accident causation.
  5. Oo & Oo (2022) [5]: Analyzed aviation weather hazards in the Yangon Flight Information Region, highlighting the dominant operational hazards of low cloud, sudden squalls, and fog in tropical monsoon aerodromes.
  6. Sládek (2025) [6]: Proposed an artificial intelligence framework for automated Terminal Aerodrome Forecast (TAF) generation, proving that machine learning can model discrete aviation categories such as wind speed and visibility thresholds.
  7. Shi et al. (2015) [7]: Formulated the Convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM) network for radar precipitation nowcasting, demonstrating the vital importance of capturing both spatial and temporal dependencies.
  8. Dueben & Bauer (2018) [9] & Rasp & Lerch (2018) [10]: Documented the design principles and post-processing strengths of deep learning ensembles in correcting numerical weather predictions.
  9. Hu, Zhang, & Li (2021) [11]: Explored Random Forest ensembles in civil aviation flight delay forecasting, validating ensemble decision tree robustness in handling noisy civil aviation datasets.
  10. Boonyuen et al. (2016) [12]: Researched the impact of GPS radio occultation and meteorological observations on numerical weather prediction, highlighting data fidelity as the core determinant of forecast skill.

07 Existing Approaches and Related Systems

Current airport weather forecasting relies primarily on two methodologies:

  • Global Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP): Models such as NOAA's Global Forecast System (GFS) and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) resolve atmospheric hydrodynamics across coarse horizontal grid resolutions (13–25 km). They fail to model microclimates formed by the runway surface, Negombo coastal lagoon, and coastal-inland thermal boundaries.
  • Manual TAF Issuance: Aerodrome meteorologists manually interpret synoptic charts, satellite imagery, and NWP output to formulate standard TAF text forecasts every 6 hours. While expert-guided, this process is labor-intensive, cannot recalculate every hour dynamically, and lacks an automated closed-loop statistical verification mechanism against subsequent METARs.

08 Research Gap

Identified Scientific Gap

Existing Sri Lankan meteorological research focuses almost exclusively on broad regional rainfall forecasting and agricultural water management [1], [2]. Conversely, international aviation studies focus on temperate mid-latitude aerodromes [4], [6]. No prior published study has:

  1. Jointly predicted the complete set of primary surface aviation parameters (wind speed/direction, temperature, barometric pressure/QNH, dew point, relative humidity, cloud status, and horizontal visibility) at a dedicated tropical airport (VCBI);
  2. Tailored machine learning architectures explicitly to the four distinct Sri Lankan monsoon regimes; and
  3. Coupled machine learning outputs with standard aerodynamic physical formulas (Density Altitude, Runway 04 Headwind/Crosswind) while maintaining an automated, continuous verification feedback loop against live METAR observations.

09 Research Problem

Commercial aviation at Bandaranaike International Airport (VCBI) lacks a localized, high-resolution, short-term (T+3h) weather forecasting mechanism capable of rapidly updating between official TAF cycles, adapting to sharp monsoon-induced changes, converting predicted meteorology into actionable flight safety parameters, and automatically measuring its own predictive error against realized ground truth observations.

10 Research Aim

To design, implement, and rigorously evaluate a hyper-local aviation weather forecasting and automated verification system tailored to Sri Lankan monsoon conditions at Bandaranaike International Airport (VCBI), delivering pilot-ready aeronautical safety metrics and continuous MLOps-driven forecast verification.

11 Research Objectives

  1. Acquire, clean, chronologically organize, and engineer domain features from over 120,000 historical VCBI METAR records (2019–2026) and ingest hourly live AWC data.
  2. Develop a hybrid Random Forest + LSTM model to forecast temperature and QNH at T+3h, translating outputs into Pressure Altitude and Density Altitude categories.
  3. Develop an ensemble XGBoost + Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) model using cyclic angle vectors to forecast runway wind speed and direction within ICAO tolerance (<2.5 kt).
  4. Develop a gradient boosted XGBoost model to classify cloud ceiling status and predict horizontal runway visibility in meters.
  5. Develop a LightGBM model combined with the August-Roche-Magnus thermodynamic equation to forecast QNH and dew point while physically deriving Relative Humidity and radiation fog risk.
  6. Establish an automated verification engine that calculates MAE, RMSE, and R² against realized observations and initiates monthly model retraining.

12 Research Questions

  • RQ1: How can recurrent sequence models (LSTM, TFT) and tree-based gradient ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM, RF) be combined to improve short-term (T+3h) aviation weather prediction over standalone baselines?
  • RQ2: How effectively can cyclic trigonometric transformations (sine/cosine) preserve angular continuity (359° to 001°) in airport wind vector forecasting?
  • RQ3: Does deriving Relative Humidity via physical thermodynamic equations (August-Roche-Magnus) prevent physical inconsistencies compared to training separate uncoupled machine learning models?
  • RQ4: Can an automated closed-loop MLOps verification pipeline effectively mitigate concept drift caused by Sri Lanka's seasonal monsoon shifts?

13 Research Methodology

The research follows an applied quantitative engineering methodology divided into six modular phases:

Phase 1: Ingestion

Chronological extraction of historical records (2019–2026) and continuous hourly telemetry ingestion from Aviation Weather Center via APScheduler background tasks.

Phase 2: Feature Engineering

Extraction of cyclic temporal features, lag variables (T-1, T-2, T-3), 6-hour rolling statistics, dew point depression, and meteorological interaction terms.

Phase 3: Model Training

Strict chronological train/test splitting (80/20) without random shuffling to avoid lookahead bias. Hyperparameter optimization using Grid Search and 3-fold cross-validation.

Phase 4: Aeronautical Translation

Application of FAA and ICAO physical equations to translate raw predicted scalars into operational indicators: DA, Runway 04 HW/CW, and fog threat indices.

Phase 5: Verification

As future METAR data arrives, prediction logs are automatically matched with ground truth to log regression metrics (MAE, RMSE, R²) and classification confusion matrices.

Phase 6: Deployment & MLOps

Decoupled microservice deployment via FastAPI and Flask, synchronized through Supabase and SQLite, with scheduled hot-reload retraining.

14 Proposed System Architecture

The system architecture follows a decoupled plugin-based service design ensuring high availability and fault isolation. The core dataflow is organized into three architectural layers:

  • Data Ingestion & Storage Tier: Live METAR parser backed by an SQLite local cache and Supabase cloud PostgreSQL database with a 48-hour data retention security net.
  • Analytical & Machine Learning Tier: Four independent model microservices (FastAPI and Flask endpoints) that load serialized model artifacts, generate inference vectors, and execute aerodynamic formulas.
  • Presentation & Verification Tier: Pilot decision portal and air traffic controllers' dashboard displaying runway crosswind alert levels, density altitude bars, and live verification error tracking.

15 Data Collection

The primary training corpus comprises over 120,000 meteorological records collected at Bandaranaike International Airport (VCBI) across the 7-year span from 2019 to 2026, secured through the Sri Lanka Department of Meteorology.

Real-time inference and verification data are harvested automatically on an hourly schedule from the Aviation Weather Center (AWC) international feed, covering runway surface wind speed, wind direction, temperature, dew point, barometric pressure (QNH), cloud cover status, and horizontal visibility.

16 Data Preprocessing and Feature Engineering

Meteorological data requires rigorous preprocessing to account for physical constraints:

  • Chronological Ordering & Imputation: All timestamps are normalized to UTC. Missing values are imputed using forward-fill to maintain temporal validity without leaking future state.
  • Cyclic Angular Encodings: Wind direction ($\theta$) and hour of day ($H$) are transformed into orthogonal sine and cosine pairs:
    Wind_x = sin(2π · θ / 360), Wind_y = cos(2π · θ / 360)
    This ensures 359° and 001° are mathematically adjacent rather than opposing extremes.
  • Dew Point Depression: Computed as (Dry Bulb Temp - Dew Point Temp), acting as a direct physical indicator of atmospheric moisture saturation and air density.
  • Rolling Trends & Interaction Variables: 3-hour and 6-hour moving averages, barometric momentum (rate of pressure change), temperature-humidity interactions, and squared relative humidity terms.

17 Machine Learning Model Development

The four specialized model pipelines were trained, tuned, and evaluated as follows:

A. Temperature & Pressure (RF + LSTM Hybrid)

Random Forest (RF) captures non-linear cross-variable interactions between humidity, wind, and lagged temperature. Concurrently, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with a 6-hour lookback window captures sequential atmospheric persistence. Outputs are combined into a joint T+3h forecast of temperature and QNH.

B. Wind Speed & Direction (XGBoost + TFT Ensemble)

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) trained via 3-fold cross-validation with tuned hyperparameters (n_estimators: 300–1000, max_depth: 5–9, learning_rate: 0.01–0.05) provides rapid 1-hour nowcasts. A Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) with 32 hidden units, 12-hour encoder lookback, and attention mechanisms models the longer 3-hour forecast horizon.

C. Cloud & Visibility (Dual XGBoost Models)

Two dedicated models: an XGBoost Classifier for cloud coverage category and an XGBoost Regressor for runway horizontal visibility distance in meters, utilizing interaction features between barometric pressure and wind speed.

D. QNH, Dew Point & RH (LightGBM + Thermodynamic Coupling)

Two separate LightGBM models forecast T+3h QNH and dew point. Relative Humidity (RH) is then derived directly using the August-Roche-Magnus thermodynamic equation:
RH = 100 · exp[(17.67 · Td / (Td + 243.5)) - (17.67 · T / (T + 243.5))]
Eliminating the error drift of an unconstrained standalone RH regressor.

20 Expected and Actual Research Contributions

The completed research delivers four tangible contributions to aviation meteorology in Sri Lanka:

1. Operational Density Altitude (DA) Modeling

Automates the conversion of predicted temperature and QNH into Pressure Altitude and Density Altitude at VCBI:
PA = (1013.25 - Predicted_QNH) · 30
ISA_Temp = 15 - [2 · (PA / 1000)]
ISA_Dev = Predicted_Temp - ISA_Temp
DA = PA + (118.8 · ISA_Dev)
Provides automated warnings for Normal, Elevated, and Critical takeoff density stages.

2. Runway-Oriented Wind Vector Decomposition

Wind direction and velocity are resolved relative to Runway 04 heading (040°):
Angle θ = |Wind_Dir - 040°|
Headwind (HW) = V · cos(θ)
Crosswind (CW) = V · sin(θ)
Gives direct situational awareness for safe approach and landing flap settings.

3. Radiation Fog Thermodynamic Warning Rule

Establishes a localized physical heuristic validated against VCBI climatology: a temperature drop ≥1.0 °C within 3 hours accompanied by QNH ≥1012.0 hPa triggers early fog and ceiling deterioration alerts.

4. Continuous MLOps Verification Feedback Loop

Unlike static academic models, this implementation provides an automated pipeline that continuously scores every prediction against subsequent real-world METAR telemetry, logging performance drift and triggering monthly hot-reload retraining.
